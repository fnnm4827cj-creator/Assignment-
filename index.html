Students Who Study More Score Higher, Data on 100,000 Students Shows

Students who studied ten hours or more a day averaged 85.8 points on their exams, compared with 53.7 for those who studied less than an hour.

By Navaneeth Sivadas

Students aged 14 to 20 who study more tend to score higher. In a dataset of 100,000 students, those who studied ten hours or more a day averaged 85.8 points, while those who studied under an hour averaged 53.7.

Students who study more score higher, and students under more stress score lower. That is the main pattern in a dataset of 100,000 students and their exam results. The average score was 62 out of 100, and 77.4% of students passed.

The numbers come from a file called student_exam_performance. It holds 44 facts about each student, such as age, family income, sleep, screen time and stress. The students are between 14 and 20 years old and the average age is 17. The file doesn't say when the exams took place or which schools the students went to, so these findings apply only to this group of students.

The main finding

Past results and study time show the biggest gaps in exam scores

The chart compares the average exam score of the lowest and highest group on four factors. The bigger the gap between the two bars, the more that factor went with different scores.

The more the students studied, the better they scored. Those who studied less than one hour a day averaged 53.7 points, while those who studied ten hours or more averaged 85.8. Most students studied about three hours a day and the average score went up with every extra hour they studied. Only 259 students, or 0.3%, studied ten hours or more, so that group is very small.

Past results, money and sleep

How students did before is a good clue on how they do now. Those who scored under 40 last time averaged 38.5 and those who scored 90 or more averaged 79. Earlier grade point average tells a similar story. Practice tests and finished assignments also went with higher scores, though not as strongly.

Money and sleep show up in the scores too. Students from low-income families scored 59.1 on average, compared with 65.5 for high-income families, a gap of 6.4 points. Students who slept under four hours scored 54.3, while those who slept ten hours or more scored 70.8. Most students slept around seven hours.

What made little difference

Going to class and managing time well went with slightly higher scores, but not by much. Attendance had a small link to scores (0.17 on a scale where 0 means no link), and time management had an even smaller one (0.11). Screen time, internet access, online learning and exercise made almost no difference.

To find this, the data was split into groups, for example by gender, and the average score of each group was compared. The groups were almost identical. Gender differed by only about 0.2 points, private tuition by about 0.1, and school type by about 0.4.

How scores were spread out

Scores spread widely. About 64.5% of students scored between 50 and 80, but 4.1% scored 90 or above and 0.5% scored below 20. The most common grade was D, at 34.9%, followed by F at 22.6% and C at 21.8%. Only 7.5% earned an A. Students who passed averaged 68.1, while those who failed averaged 40.8.

The reporting question

Why do students under more stress score lower, and does studying more lead to better marks? The data shows both patterns but cannot answer either question by itself.

What the data shows

Students who studied under one hour a day averaged 53.7 points, against 85.8 for those who studied ten hours or more, a gap of more than 30 points. Easy exams averaged 15 points higher than hard exams. Scores hardly differed by background details such as gender, tuition or school type, but they differed a lot by study habits, past results and exam conditions.

What the data does not prove

The data shows patterns, but it can't tell us why they happen. It can't explain why stressed students score lower, or prove that studying more leads to better marks. The file also doesn't say when the exams took place or which schools the students went to, so the findings describe these 100,000 students and not all students everywhere.

About the data

Methodology and limitations

The numbers come from a file called student_exam_performance, which holds 44 facts about each of 100,000 students aged 14 to 20. To find the patterns, the data was split into groups, for example by gender, and the average score of each group was compared.

About 52,000 entries are missing from the file, including 9.9% of attendance figures and 7.8% of previous grade point averages. These gaps were left empty and not filled in with guesses. The file also doesn't say where the data came from, so the findings describe these 100,000 students and not all students everywhere.
